A GUIDE TO OUR EXPERIMENTS

How information gets lost on its way to a decision

Organizations and AI systems collect more reports than anyone at the top can read, so reports get summarized before they reach the people who decide. We ran experiments to find out what gets lost in that summarizing, and how to stop losing the parts that matter. This page explains the experiments and the results.

THE PROBLEM

Every stage of summarizing drops something.

AI systems increasingly run as teams of many small programs called agents. The agents gather far more information than any person can read. So the information is summarized in stages: many field reports become a few regional summaries, the summaries become one briefing, and the briefing reaches a decision desk.

At every stage, something gets dropped. There is only so much room. Which means every one of these systems contains a summarizing rule: the rule that decides which reports keep their place at each stage and which get dropped. Almost nobody chooses this rule on purpose. It is usually an accident of whatever software was used.

THE EXPERIMENT

We ran the same disaster twice and changed only one rule.

We built a computer simulation of a disaster region: 64 communities, 16,299 simulated people. Software agents survey the communities and file reports. The reports climb four stages to a decision desk, and the desk sends help to the four communities it believes need it most.

We ran the simulation twice. The agents made exactly the same visits, in the same order, both times — this is provable from the logs, down to the byte. The only thing that changed between the two runs was the rule deciding which reports keep their place at each stage.

The first summarizing rule we tested is called "the loudest get in." Most real systems use it without knowing it: reports that recently led to action keep their place, and the rest get crowded out. The second rule is called "neighbors carry each other," and it is copied from fungal networks, which survive by connecting neighbors: reports from nearby communities merge and travel upward together in one shared slot.

WHAT WE FOUND

One rule erased 26 communities from view. The other kept all 64.

Under "the loudest get in," 26 of the 64 communities disappeared from the decision desk's view — 7,364 people. The agents kept visiting those communities the whole time. Their reports kept being filed. Every report died at the same stage of the climb, every time, because those communities had lost their standing in it.

We then tried the obvious fix: sending extra agents back to the dropped communities. It made things worse. The new reports were dropped at the same stage as the old ones, and the diverted visits slowed coverage for everyone else.

The fix that worked was switching the summarizing rule from "the loudest get in" to "neighbors carry each other." Every community stayed visible, and three quarters of the lost performance came back. The cost of the switch was nothing: same agents, same visits, same budget.

THEN WE TESTED IT WITH REAL AI AGENTS

The same failure appeared, along with two surprises.

Simulations use simple stand-ins, so we repeated the ideas with real Claude agents: 172 of them across three experiments, reading reports, forming judgments, and making decisions on their own.

The first surprise was reassuring. A single capable agent, given enough room to work, protected the quiet emergencies on its own — it read past dramatic language and found the severe cases described in dry, boring reports. The failures we study appear when budgets get tight and structure takes over.

So we tightened the budget, organized six survey agents three different ways, and ran a five-round disaster with planted emergencies. The team that followed recent action went blind: within two rounds it stopped reading half the region, and it completely missed one of the two emergencies. The team organized with systematic coverage plus sideways tips caught both emergencies before they erupted — field agents warned each other about trouble outside their own assignments without being told to.

The second surprise: decision-makers dropped a previously favored community the moment reports said it had recovered. Favoritism appeared only while the favorite still looked needy. Clear evidence cured it.

WHY IT MATTERS

The summarizing rule can be chosen on purpose. We built the tools for choosing it.

Every organization that gathers more information than its leaders can read has a version of this problem. Disaster response. Satellite monitoring. Public health. And every AI system built from many agents, which is quickly becoming most of them.

What we built makes a system's summarizing rule visible and testable. It is three things: a simulator that shows which voices a system will lose before it is deployed, a catalog of organizing rules borrowed from living networks, and measured evidence for which rules keep information alive on its way to a decision.

The rules come from organisms because organisms have been solving this exact problem — moving resources through a network on a tight budget — far longer than we have.

Everything above is reproducible. The predictions were written down before each experiment ran, the runs are logged and replayable, and the predictions that failed are published alongside the ones that held.
