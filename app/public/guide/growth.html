The complete guide to our experiments

Can AI fleets work better by growing like living things?

We are testing one idea: teams of AI agents work better when they are organized by growth rules copied from living networks. This page collects every experiment we have run, names the growth rule each one tested, and shows what that rule did to the people in the simulation. Every number comes from logged runs that replay exactly.

The idea

Living networks have already solved the problem AI fleets are hitting now.

AI systems increasingly run as fleets: many small programs, called agents, that gather information, summarize it, and pass it upward to whoever decides. Every fleet lives on a budget. There is never enough reading time, never enough room in a summary, never enough crews on the ground.

A budget forces rules. Which report keeps its place in a crowded summary? Which town gets read this round? Which connection is worth paying for? Most software answers these questions with defaults nobody chose on purpose.

Living networks answer the same questions with growth. A slime mould thickens the paths that carry food and lets the rest fade. A fungal network fuses neighboring strands so resources and warnings travel together. A root tip sends probes toward whatever it is missing. A lichen pairs two organisms whose weaknesses cancel. Each of these is a rule that survival has tested for millions of years. We translated eight of them into exact, runnable code.

How we test

The same world runs twice, and only the growth rule changes.

Every experiment is a simulated world with real population numbers: communities, storms, emergencies, rescue crews. We run the world at least twice. Between runs we change exactly one rule, the growth rule under test, and hold everything else identical, including budgets, which are matched and checked in code.

The worlds repeat exactly. The same starting seed produces the same event log, byte for byte, every time, and every visual page on this site replays those logs instead of re-running anything.

Before each experiment we write down what we expect to happen and save it in the project's public history. Ten of the fourteen predictions in our newest three experiments turned out wrong, and the wrong ones are published beside the right ones. Being wrong in public is what makes the surviving rules trustworthy.

Experiment one: the two shapes

A flood hit 64 communities. The way reports were summarized decided who got help.

Picture a flooded region: 64 communities, 16,299 people. Survey teams visit every community, every day, and file reports on who needs help. Far too many reports exist for one office to read, so they pass through four rounds of summarizing on the way up. The office at the top sends help to the four communities its briefing says need it most.

Every round of summarizing has limited room, so a rule somewhere in the software decides which reports keep their place. Real disaster agencies work this way, and so does every system built from many AI agents: information is summarized in stages before anyone acts on it. We ran the same flood twice and changed only that one rule.

Growth rules in this experiment

Slime mould Reports that recently led to action keep their summary slot, and the rest get crowded out. 26 of 64 communities vanished from the view of the office sending help.

Fungal network Reports from neighboring communities merge and travel together in one slot. All 64 communities stayed visible, at the same cost.

Root tip Send extra survey agents back to the communities that dropped out. This made things worse: the new reports died at the same stage as the old ones.

The survey teams made exactly the same visits, in the same order, in both runs; the logs prove it down to the byte. Under the slime-mould rule the office could see 8,935 of the 16,299 people. Under the fungal merging rule it could see every one of them.

Experiment two: the bridge

A city monitors an aging bridge through 256 sensors. Only the software that recorded what it discarded could still prove the bridge safe.

A city watches an aging bridge through 256 sensors bolted to its cables and decks. Software condenses those readings in stages: 256 readings become 64 summaries, then 16 findings, then 8 safety claims, then the one sentence the city acts on: the bridge is safe. In the simulation, 21,000 people live in the districts those claims cover.

When someone asks why the bridge is called safe, the answer has to walk backward from that sentence, link by link, down to a real sensor reading. A claim with no surviving trail governs its people on faith. Machine-made conclusions are assembled this way everywhere: bridge reports, medical summaries, intelligence briefings. We built the linking two ways on the same budget and asked which claims could still show their proof after damage.

Growth rules in this experiment

Slime mould Every conclusion keeps one thickened link to its strongest source. After damage and repair, zero broken evidence paths were restored, permanently.

Fungal network Links spread across several neighboring sources. 82 percent of conclusions kept a path to their evidence, against 60 percent for the strongest-support version.

Coral Every discarded link leaves a small permanent record of what was cut. Replaying those records restored 100 percent of the broken paths.

The same damage hit both versions: the same third of the summarizing workers destroyed, then the same small repair budget for each. The version that spread its links and recorded every cut healed completely. The strongest-support version had written nothing down when it discarded links, so its breaks are permanent: 14,420 of its 21,000 people ended the run under claims nobody can check.

Two honest warnings came from the same runs. An attacker who studies the spread-out version and destroys its busiest junctions first collapses it below what random damage does to the strongest-support version. And redundancy stopped paying early: past 1.5 times the minimum wiring, extra links bought nothing.

Experiment three: the boat

A flood cut off a coast of 48 settlements, and one rescue boat had to choose where to sail on conflicting information.

A storm floods a coast of 48 settlements and 18,000 people. The rescue service has two ways of knowing where people are in the water: satellite pictures from above, and phone calls from the ground. The storm blinds the satellite and cuts the phone lines in exactly the places it hits hardest, so both sources go quiet where the trouble is worst.

There is one rescue boat, and once it commits to a passage it cannot turn around. We ran the same storms three times and changed only one thing: how the rescue office combines its two sources before choosing where the boat goes.

Growth rules in this experiment

Bacterial swarm Satellite readings and phone reports blend into one map the moment they arrive, and the desk herds toward the strongest signal. Cheap in ordinary storms, and it got worse the moment the phone reports began to exaggerate, because a fused map leaves no channel to doubt.

Lichen The satellite and the phone reports stay separate partners all the way to the decision. This cost about 3 percent in ordinary storms, and it was the only desk that improved when one witness lied.

Root tip When the two witnesses disagree about the top target, fly out and look before committing. During the poisoned season this desk spent 69 percent of its missions verifying and rescued 10,824 people, while the lichen desk rescued 23,856.

Two of our predictions died here. The boat never once sailed into empty water across 32 simulated storm seasons, because a stale map underestimates a growing flood. And checking, which we expected to win, lost badly: disagreement triggers checking, corruption creates disagreement, so the checking bill arrives exactly when checking is least affordable.

Experiment four: the map

Emergencies with deadlines hit 40 towns. The rescue office that kept rerouting its walking crews saved the most people.

A rural region: 40 towns, 15,000 people, and six rescue crews who travel on foot. Emergencies strike on their own schedule, and each one has a deadline; reach those people in time, or they go unhelped, permanently. The dispatch office plans every route from a map that refreshes only every 20 minutes, so it is always navigating by a slightly old photograph.

We ran the same emergencies three times and changed only what a crew already walking does when a fresh map arrives.

Growth rules in this experiment

Vascular A crew's current destination stays locked, like a plant's growing tip, and only the later stops adjust. Zero wasted kilometres, 98 percent of promises kept, and ten emergencies expired inside their deadlines.

Finish the route Crews complete the plan they started, then take new orders. This desk served 7,573 people.

Turn them around Every fresh map may redirect every crew immediately, even over ground already covered. This desk served 8,517 people, wasted 11 percent of its walking, and met every deadline.

Plan stability turned out to measure the wrong thing. The tidiest desks scored a perfect 1.000 on it while ten emergencies waited past their deadlines. A desk with a perfect live map, run as the ceiling, turned its crews around three to four times more than anyone and served the most people of all.

Then we used real AI agents

We handed the same kind of disaster to teams of real AI agents, organized three different ways.

Everything above ran with simple stand-in agents, so we repeated the ideas with 172 real Claude agents reading reports, forming judgments, and deciding on their own. The main test: six survey agents covering a disaster region of 24 towns across five rounds, with two planted emergencies and only enough reading budget to cover half the towns each round. The only difference between the three teams was the growth rule that organized them.

Growth rules in this experiment

Vascular Six agents rotate through fixed districts, like ranked branches under one trunk. This fleet read 20 of 24 towns: reliable, never blind, never fast.

Slime mould The fleet reads wherever recent action was. At a tight budget it collapsed to 12 of 24 towns and left one erupted emergency unread for three straight rounds.

Fungal network Systematic coverage, plus sideways tips passed between agents. This fleet held 20 of 24 towns and aided both emergencies before they erupted.

The first surprise was reassuring. A single capable agent with room to work protected the quiet emergencies by itself. The failures appeared when the budget got tight and the pipeline's rules took over, which is exactly where the growth rules differ from each other.

The second surprise: every decision desk dropped a previously favored community the moment reports said it had recovered, so favoritism lived on ambiguity and clear evidence cured it. The standing rule after two runs: count how much of your territory your fleet can actually read each round, because reinforcement-style attention is an asset above that line and a liability below it.

What we test next

The open questions need more computing power than a laptop.

The sharpest unknowns are thresholds. At exactly what reading budget does slime-mould reinforcement flip from asset to liability? How much checking can a root-tip desk afford before doubt consumes the boat? Where does the bridge's extra wiring stop buying repair? Finding a threshold takes hundreds of runs per point, and local AI models on dedicated graphics hardware make those runs affordable.

Two bigger questions wait behind the thresholds. The first: do seven genuinely different AI models watching the same world fail together, or catch each other? Our deterministic version of that experiment cut joint failures from 43 percent to zero by preserving disagreement, and real models are the honest test. The second: does the tendril rule, still untested, earn its card on the scoreboard above?

Everything above is reproducible. The predictions were written down before each experiment ran, the runs are logged and replayable, and the predictions that failed are published beside the ones that held.
