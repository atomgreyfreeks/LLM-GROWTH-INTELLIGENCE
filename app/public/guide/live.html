The live runs, explained

We gave three disasters to fleets of real AI agents, organized by growth rules from living networks.

This whole project tests one idea: teams of AI agents work better when the rules organizing them are copied from living networks such as slime moulds, fungal networks, lichens, and root tips. The earlier experiments used scripted stand-in agents. This page reports the live runs: 68 real Claude agents, three disaster scenarios, every prediction written down and committed publicly before any agent ran, and every score computed mechanically against a sealed answer key.

How a live run works

The growth rule is enforced by the machinery, and the agents do the judging.

Each experiment builds a fixed simulated world: settlements, sensor reports, storms, emergencies, all generated from a committed seed. The fleet's growth rule — which reports merge, which sources get cited, which crews may turn around — is enforced mechanically by the harness, exactly as it was in the scripted versions. The real agents read the world's documents, form judgments, and make the choices. The same world runs once per organizing rule, and the rules are the only difference between runs.

Ten of our fourteen predictions for the scripted versions of these three experiments had already failed, and the live runs continued the pattern: three more predictions failed here, each in an instructive way, and the failures are reported below next to the successes.

The flooded coast

Phone callers exaggerated a coastal flood, and the fleet forced to fly out and double-check reached a third fewer people.

Growth systems in this run, and how each fared

Bacterial swarm Satellite readings and phone calls merge into one map before the dispatcher sees anything. Succeeded, 207 of a possible 207 people reached in the lying season — because the agent doing the merging discounted the lies on its own.

Lichen The satellite and the phone log stay separate witnesses all the way to the decision. Close behind, 188 of 207, losing 19 people to two judgment slips.

Root tip Whenever the two witnesses disagree strongly, the first boat slot must be spent flying out to verify. Failed as predicted: 131 of 207, with every verification flight spent on the same exaggerating settlement.

The world: twelve coastal settlements, six storm rounds, two boat slots per round. The first three rounds were honest, and all three fleets made identical, perfect picks. In the last three rounds, three settlements' phone callers exaggerated their numbers several times over, and the settlement that was truly worst went silent.

The prediction that keeping witnesses separate would win under lies failed, and the reason matters. The merged-map fleet's fusion step was itself a capable agent: it read the satellite's clear low readings at the exaggerating settlements and cut their claims down before the dispatcher ever saw a number. A merged map is only as dangerous as the merger is mechanical. The prediction that mandatory double-checking would spend its rescue capacity exactly when checking is least affordable held completely, with a real agent holding the rule.

The bridge office

Half a bridge-inspection office was destroyed, and only the team that kept records of its discarded sources stayed perfect.

Growth systems in this run, and how each fared

Fungal network Each conclusion cites several neighboring sources instead of one. Succeeded: 8 of 8 evidence trails answered correctly after the damage.

Coral Every discarded source leaves a small permanent record of what was cut. The records are what made the perfect repair possible.

Slime mould Each conclusion keeps only its single strongest source. Predicted to collapse to 3 of 8 after damage; it survived at 7 of 8, because its writers smuggled source names into their prose.

The world: a city monitors an aging bridge through sixteen sensor reports. Four engineer agents summarize them into zone findings, a chief agent writes the safety claims, and then two of the four findings are deleted. A fresh auditor agent must then answer eight questions of the form "which sensor report supports this statement?", with a capped number of archive lookups.

Before the damage, both teams answered all eight questions. After the damage, the record-keeping team stayed perfect, and the strongest-support team missed exactly one. The predicted collapse never came, because real summarizers preserve the trail in their sentences even when the citation rule cuts it: one finding named three sensors in its prose despite citing only one. The single question the strongest-support team lost was the one fact its writer had left out of the prose too — when a fact fell out of both the sentences and the citations, it was gone for good.

The deadline region

Five flash floods hit a sixteen-town region, every fleet reached all of them, and one agent legally outsmarted its own commitment rule.

Growth systems in this run, and how each fared

Tree trunk A crew's route may only be rewritten once it is finished. Tied at 5 of 5 emergencies — its dispatcher discovered that assigning one-stop routes keeps every decision fresh, legally.

Vascular A crew's current stop and next stop stay locked; only the stop after that may change. Tied at 5 of 5 emergencies.

Reroute Any crew may be turned around at any time, at the cost of a lost round of walking. Tied at 5 of 5 emergencies, and it never paid a single walking round — it rerouted futures, never the crew already walking.

The world: sixteen towns, four walking crews, six rounds, and a briefing that is always one round out of date. Five flash floods appeared on a fixed schedule, each with a hard deadline three rounds out. All three fleets served all five emergencies and all 1,487 people inside their windows, so the prediction that locked and committed fleets would miss emergencies failed.

Two things from the transcripts are worth more than the tie. First, in round two, before anything unusual happened, the finish-the-route dispatcher chose to assign only one stop and wrote down why: a one-stop route ends every round, so the office gets a fresh decision every round. The rule restricted rewriting plans, so the agent stopped making long plans. Second, one honest flaw is on the record: our checker for the finish-the-route arm wrongly rejected some multi-stop assignments, which pushed that fleet further toward short routes than its own strategy already had. The full account is in the results file.

What the three runs teach together

Growth rules decide the outcome when the budget binds, and capable agents rescue any structure when it does not.

One pattern runs through all three scenarios. Wherever the organizing rule truly bound — the mandatory verification flights that could not be skipped — the scripted version's failure replicated exactly with real agents, and the fleet lost a third of the people it could have reached. Wherever the rule left any slack, a capable agent found the escape: the fusion agent judged instead of averaging, the summarizers carried their sources in prose, the committed dispatcher shortened its commitments, and the rerouting dispatcher never paid for a turn.

This is the same law the earlier 99-agent fleet runs found from the other direction: at a generous reading budget the fleet organizations tied, and at a tight budget the reinforcement fleet went blind while rotation and fungal coverage survived. The choice of growth system matters most exactly when capacity is scarce — which is precisely when the stakes are highest, and precisely the condition the next round of experiments will bind harder.

Everything above is reproducible. The worlds, the answer keys, and the predictions were committed before any agent ran; the run logs and mechanical scorecards sit next to the results files in the repository's documentation folders; and the predictions that failed are published beside the ones that held.
